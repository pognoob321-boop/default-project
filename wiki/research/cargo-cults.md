# Cargo Cults

## Summary

Cargo cults were spiritual and political movements among indigenous Melanesians following Western colonization in the early 20th century. Followers believed an abundance of Western goods ("cargo") would arrive, brought by their ancestors, if they performed the right rituals — including imitating colonial/military practices like building mock airstrips, marching, and flag-raising. The term first appeared in print in Pacific Islands Monthly (Nov 1945). The best-known surviving example is the John Frum movement on Tanna island, Vanuatu, still active today.

## Key Facts

- Earliest documented movement: Tuka Movement, Fiji, 1885
- Rooted in Melanesian "big man" gift-exchange culture + colonial "value dominance"
- Prophets foretold cataclysm → ancestors return → cargo arrives via canoe → sail → steamship → WWII planes
- Practitioners "value dominance": foreigners controlled the goods; spiritual beings intended them for islanders
- WWII Pacific theater accelerated cults (military bases, airdrops)
- Term now criticized by anthropologists as pejorative/misapplied bundle; largely fell out of academic favor after 1970s (post-independence)
- 1967 photo: John Frum ceremonial cross, Tanna island

## Feynman's "Cargo Cult Science" (metaphor)

- 1974 Caltech commencement address; final chapter of "Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!"
- Islanders built mock airstrips, wooden headphones, control towers, landing signals — copying the *form* of aviation without the *principle*
- Applied to science: researchers who mimic scientific method/appearance but lack "scientific integrity" (e.g., not reporting results they dislike, fooling themselves)
- Widely reused in tech/business/education as "copying rituals of success without understanding mechanics"

- Derivative concept for speeches/essays: "cargo cult thinking" = surface mimicry without substance
