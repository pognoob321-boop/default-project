# Grade 9 Test Crunch: Supplements, Fruits, and Football — Evidence Check

Created: 2026-10-17. Requested by the user (grade 9 student, Cambodia, football player, large upcoming exam). Deliverable: ranked supplement verdict + fruit ranking + soccer fuel/hydration numbers + timing plan.

## Executive verdict

**No supplement reliably raises exam scores in a healthy, non-deficient adolescent.** The ranking of levers is unchanged from [focus-study-work-ethic](focus-study-work-ethic.md): sleep (8–9 h) > retrieval practice + spacing > food adequacy > hydration > any supplement. Everything below is a *floor-raising* move, not a score-multiplier. Anything marketed as a score-multiplier for a 15-year-old is selling something.

**The one genuinely under-served thing in this specific case is micronutrient status, and the evidence says it must be *tested*, not guessed** (see the iron section — the intuitive answer is wrong).

## 1. Supplements — ranked by evidence tier

### Tier 1 — actually worth taking

| Item | Dose | Timing | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Creatine monohydrate** (plain, unflavoured) | 3 g/day | any time, with a meal | Best-documented sports supplement in teens. 2026 Cureus systematic review (PMID 42124755, 5 human studies 2015–2025): well tolerated, no consistent renal/hepatic/cardiometabolic signal, no serious AEs. Performance effect in adolescents = **inconsistent** (2025 review, PMC10063391: 85% of studies were soccer players or swimmers, overall quality poor, "no consistent findings"). Cognitive claim is **weak** — 2024 Behavioural Brain Research systematic review (doi 10.1016/j.bbr.2024.114982) found the cognitive-effect literature "fails to support the theoretical basis." |

Read this honestly: **creatine is safe, not magic.** The teen-specific RCT evidence for *performance* is genuinely poor. It is still the correct default because the safety file is clean, it costs almost nothing, and the 2026 adolescent review explicitly frames it as a *last* step after fixing energy availability, carbs, protein, hydration and sleep.

- **Cost reality (Cambodia):** plain creatine monohydrate is not a Lucky Supermarket shelf item. Online/pharmacy only, and imported powder is the buyable form. No kilo of flavored "teen" creatine. Check third-party tested (NSF/Creatine Monohydrate screening) if imported.
- **Water weight:** expect +0.5–1.5 kg in the first 1–2 weeks. Say this out loud before starting so it isn't a panic.

### Tier 2 — cheap insurance, conditionally rational

| Item | Dose | Timing | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Multivitamin** (RDA-level, ~100% DV) | 1/day | with the biggest meal | Weak on its own in an adequate diet. Justified here specifically because a Cambodian teen diet is **rice-dominant** — a low-micronutrient-density pattern. This is *insurance against a known structural gap*, not a cognitive enhancer. |
| **Vitamin D3** | 1000–2000 IU/day | morning, with fat | Only helps if deficient. Defensible given indoor school + football-only sun exposure. Cheap (~$1–2). |

### Tier 3 — do not self-prescribe

- **Iron.** See §2. Counter-productive without a test.
- **Fish oil / omega-3.** Existing protocol tier: cognition in healthy teens = **weak, effect ≈ null**. Only justified if he eats essentially no fish — and then eat fish instead, it's cheaper.
- **Magnesium.** Only if intake is deficient. Reasonable on a rice-heavy diet, but this is a sleep-quality lever, and sleep is already lever #1 in absolute terms.

### Tier 0 — actively harmful in this situation

- **Energy drinks** (Club X, BC, Coca-Cola Energy, Red Bull). AAP: stimulants in energy drinks "have no place in the diet of children and adolescents"; adolescents should get **no energy drinks and ≤100 mg caffeine/day**. Health Canada ceiling 2.5 mg/kg/day. Single cans report 100–300 mg, some >500 mg. 46% of US caffeine overdoses (2007) were under-19. Mechanism against this use case: sleep disruption + the caffeine/sugar crash + dehydration.
- **Pre-workouts.** Creatine + stimulants + undisclosed blends. Same caffeine problem plus unregulated content.
- **"Focus" / nootropic blends, racetams, alpha-GPC stacks.** `nootropics.md` covers the evidence; nothing there clears a teen-safety bar for a two-week exam sprint.
- **Fasting / skipped meals.** Puberty + a training load + a test = do the opposite.

## 2. The Cambodia micronutrient trap — the intuitive answer is wrong

This is the most decision-relevant finding in the research and it contradicts the common "Cambodian kid = anemic = give iron" assumption.

**National Micronutrient Survey linked to CDHS-2014 (PMID 27338454, n=1512):**
- Anemia: **53% of children**, 43% of women.
- But iron deficiency: **<10% of children**. Vitamin A, B12 deficiency: **<10%**. Iron-deficiency anemia: **<5%** in children over 2.
- Hemoglobin disorders (Hb-E etc.): **>50% of the population**.
- The modifiable drivers were **zinc deficiency, hookworm infection, folic acid deficiency** — and **over 40% of anemia was not nutritional at all**.

**Counter-finding:** a WFP school-meal cluster-RCT (PMID 26751473, n=2440 aged 6–16) measured **51% iron deficiency** by inflammation-adjusted ferritin/TfR criteria, vs 15.6% anemia. So the two studies disagree on iron status and the WFP trial found fortified rice moved vitamin A (4–5× lower risk of low VA) but **barely moved hemoglobin/iron** (+0.8 g/L at 3 months, gone by 6 months).

**Why this matters for the plan:**
1. **Do not buy an iron pill blind.** With >50% of the population carrying a hemoglobin disorder, iron dosing without a diagnosis is a bad bet, and iron also blocks zinc absorption — zinc is the deficiency actually implicated in the national survey.
2. **Do get tested** if he looks pale, tires fast, gets dizzy, or has poor concentration beyond what sleep explains. A clinic blood test is cheap in Cambodia and beats any guess.
3. **Deworming is the highest-yield cheap move** for anemia in this population, per the survey authors' own conclusion. Twice yearly is the national protocol; local clinics/pharmacies stock albendazole.

## 3. Fruits — ranked for a Cambodian exam + football context

### The real evidence base is thin, and mostly about one fruit

The only acute controlled cognition trial in children: **wild blueberry drink, 200 ml ≈ 253 mg anthocyanins ≈ 240 g/1½ cups fresh blueberries**, kids 7–10, better verbal memory + attention reaction time at 2 h, no reading effect (PMID 30327868, RCT n=54). Everything else in the "brain food" genre is either adult, geriatratic, or listicle.

**So the honest ranking is not "best fruit for the brain" — it's "best fruit per riel for this job."**

| Rank | Fruit | Why it earns the slot | Cambodia reality (2026 field prices) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Banana** (ចេក) | Fast cheap carbs for pre-training and pre-exam; B6 (nerve sheath) + potassium (nerve signalling); low GI. `optimal-diet.md` carb #2. | $0.25–0.50/bunch, year-round |
| 2 | **Papaya** (ល្ហុង) | Vitamin C, water, easy to eat in volume without thinking about it. | $0.50–1.00, year-round |
| 3 | **Mango** (ស្វាយ) | Vitamin C + high carb for hard training days. Keo Romeat season is Mar–Jun. | $0.50–2.00/kg, Mar–Jun |
| 4 | **Watermelon / coconut** (ឪឡឹក) | Pure hydration volume for football in Cambodian heat — the actual job here. | $0.50–1.50, hot season |
| 5 | **Guava / dragon fruit** (ស្រកនាគ) | Very high vitamin C, cheap, novel enough to not get boring. | $0.50–1.50, year-round |

**If blueberries are actually available:** they are the one fruit with a positive child RCT, but at realistic Cambodian prices they will not be a volume food. If a shop has them, buy them as a treat; do not restructure the diet around them.

### The fruit caveat nobody prints

- **Total dose, not fruit type, is the main variable.** 3–4 portions/day (~250–400 g) is the working target. Beyond that, high-GI fruit (mango, lychee, durian, banana) becomes a blood-sugar rollercoaster that *undermines* the focus it's supposed to support.
- **Whole fruit, not juice.** Fibre is the reason the sugar arrives slowly.
- **Timing:** high-GI fruit pre-training or pre-exam. Low-GI fruit (berries, guava) as a between-block snack when the stomach is empty.

## 4. Football (soccer) — the numbers

Sources: NSCA Coach 11.2.1 soccer module; *Nutritional Considerations to Optimize Performance* (ACSM ESSM youth soccer review, ovid.com ESM.72); *Macronutrient Intake in Soccer Players — A Meta-Analysis* (PMC6627126); *Sport Nutrition for Young Athletes* (PMC3805623); ACSM Position Stand: Exercise and Fluid Replacement.

### Fuel
- **Carbohydrate: 5–10 g/kg/day.** A 50 kg teen = **250–500 g carbs/day**. White rice is a legitimate delivery system — this is a calories-and-rice problem, not a supplement problem.
- **Before a match: 8 g/kg/day the day prior**, and explicitly avoid a low ~3 g/kg/day day before (meta-analysis). So the day before a big match is a big rice day.
- **During: 30–60 g carbs/hr for >1 h** of sustained exercise. No adolescent-specific published recommendation exists (the youth review flags this gap). Practical school-player version: a banana and water, not a sports drink.
- **Protein: 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day.** 50 kg = **70–100 g/day**. Important nuance the general advice gets wrong: the *adolescent-specific* nitrogen-balance figure is **lower, 1.2–1.4 g/kg/day** (youth soccer review). Plant-based players need the **upper** end (1.6–2.2) to compensate for lower digestibility — rice + beans combined beats either alone. Also note real-world soccer intakes cluster at 5.7 g/kg carbs, i.e. **below** the 6–10 target, so most players under-fuel.
- **Recovery food within 30 min, again within 1–2 h**, containing both protein and carbs.

### Hydration (the Cambodian heat problem)
- **During: ~13 mL/kg body weight.** 50 kg = ~650 mL over a session.
- **After: ~4 mL/kg, or ~1.5 L per kg of body weight lost.** Weigh yourself before/after once and do the math.
- **Sodium is not optional in heat.** Sweat sodium varies **0.2–12.5 g/L** — a 60× range between people. Drinking plain water only dilutes blood sodium → **hyponatremia**, which the NSCA module flags as a real risk. In practice: water with a pinch of salt, salted coconut water, or a sports drink. Acidulated (salty) drinks also improve voluntary intake and fluid retention.
- Sports drink spec if buying: 6% carbohydrate, 20–30 mEq/L sodium chloride.
- **A note on bottled water as a Cambodian default:** in a country of untreated water, water safety is a real constraint on "drink 2–3 L of water." Boiled, filtered, or sealed is the sensible default — this is a practical override, not a nutrition claim.

## 4b. Shelf audit — what's already on the table

A photo of the user's existing supplement shelf was reviewed. Five products:

| Product | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Kirkland Calcium 600 mg + Vitamin D3** (USP) | Keep, conditionally. Teen calcium RDA is 1300 mg/day and most Cambodian diets fall short — but check the label for total D3 per tablet first, see overlap below. |
| **Nature's Bounty Calcium-Magnesium-Zinc + D3** | Keep. Useful zinc and magnesium (both real gaps on a rice-dominant diet). **But it duplicates the Kirkland calcium** — do not run both daily, or calcium stacks past 1300 mg for no benefit. |
| **Blackmores Vitamin E 1000 IU** | **Delete.** Confirmed dose: d-alpha-tocopherol 670 mg ≡ 1000 IU per capsule. User was taking **3/day = 3000 IU/day**, which is **~134× the adult RDA of 22.4 IU (15 mg)**. Fat-soluble, so it accumulates. SELECT (JAMA 2011, n=35,533) randomized **400 IU/day** — the common retail dose — and found a statistically significant **+17% prostate cancer risk** in healthy men (HR 1.17, 99% CI 1.004–1.36, P=.008). Caveat kept honest: SELECT enrolled men aged 50+ followed ~7 years, so it is not a direct read on months of use in a 15-year-old. The conclusion is still "stop" — zero proven benefit, a documented harm signal in males, and a dose far above any studied. |
| **Nature's Aid Trail Booster — Lion's Mane Mushroom Complex** | Interesting, but not for this sprint. Lion's mane has genuine RCT support for cognition in **older adults / mild cognitive impairment** (Mori 2009, Nihonyan 2010) — the positive trials ran **16 weeks**, so it cannot help a 2-week exam sprint even if it works. No adolescent data. Blends are understudied and unverified for content. Low harm, near-zero expected benefit before the test. |
| **Nature's Aid Multivitamin+ FOR WOMEN** | Wrong product for him. Note it also typically carries **iron** — which is exactly the nutrient §2 says not to self-dose in a population where >50% carry a hemoglobin disorder. Do not swap it in. |

#### Three things the labels revealed that guessing did not

1. **Nature's Bounty serving size is 3 capsules, not 1.** The label reads "Serving Size 3 Capsules / 33 servings." Taking one softgel delivers **one third** of the printed dose — 333 mg calcium, not 1,000 mg. He was getting far less than the front of the bottle implies.
2. **Both Nature's Aid bottles and the Kirkland bottle say "Keep out of the reach of children."** The Nature's Bounty directions say "For adults." Every product on this shelf is labelled for adults and he is 15. This is the single most defensible reason to simplify the shelf, independent of any evidence tier.
3. **The "lion's mane" product is a 10-mushroom blend**, not a lion's mane supplement. Reishi, chaga, cordyceps, turkey tail and shiitake have no controlled cognition trials at all. Only one ingredient in the bottle has any human research, and it is the wrong form and wrong duration.
4. **Added sugar:** 2.5 g per 2 gummies in *both* gummy products = 5 g/day. Trivial next to a soda, but it is sugar that arrives with no fibre.

#### Corrected stack

| Action | Product |
|---|---|
| **STOP** | Blackmores Vitamin E (3,000 IU/day) |
| **STOP** | Nature's Aid 10-mushroom blend (novelty, no relevant evidence) |
| **KEEP** | Kirkland Calcium + D3 ×1 tab **with food** |
| **SIMPLIFY** | Drop the Nature's Bounty cap (1/3 serving, magnesium oxide, duplicate calcium) or take the **full 3-cap serving** as labelled and drop the Kirkland instead. Do not run all three calcium sources. |
| **KEEP or DROP** | Nature's Aid Multivitamin+ for Women — no iron, decent B/C/A coverage, but wrong marketing for him and 500 mg more calcium. Harmless. |
| **ADD** | **Creatine monohydrate 3 g/day** |
| **ADD** | **Vitamin D3 to reach ~1,000–2,000 IU/day** — the labels show he is at 800 |

### The pattern across three turns

He owns five products. The combined effect of all five is: a small multivitamin's worth of coverage, **1,433 mg of calcium he did not need**, **3,000 IU of vitamin E he should not take**, and **zero creatine**. The bottles are not the problem and they were never going to be the solution. §5's floor items — sleep, 5–10 g/kg carbs, 1.4–2.0 g/kg protein, salted water, retrieval practice — remain where all the value is.

## 5. Timing plan (assumes the test is ~2 weeks out)

**Days 14–10 — build the floor (highest ROI, do it first)**
1. Fix sleep: 8–9 h, fixed wake time, phone out of the room. Largest single lever, and it is free.
2. Start creatine 3 g/day + a RDA multivitamin. Measure and record weight. Tell someone the water-weight number in advance.
3. Carbs up: 5–10 g/kg/day. Practically, more rice at lunch and dinner than feels normal.
4. Deworming visit if he has never been dewormed, or if there are any signs worth flagging to a clinic.

**Days 9–5 — swap the study technique, add the football**
5. Retrieval practice (close the book, write what you know), 2–3 × 90-min blocks at peak hours, phone in another room. Highlight-and-reread is worth roughly nothing per Dunlosky's 700-paper review.
6. Keep training. Drop intensity ~30% the last 3 days before the exam to protect sleep and glycogen.
7. 2–3 fruit portions/day. Bananas + papaya as the default two.

**Days 4–2 — protect, do not add**
8. **Start nothing new.** Any new supplement, any unfamiliar food, any big volume change = GI risk on exam day. This is the single most concrete rule in this page.
9. Hydrate deliberately, eat normally, no energy drinks. Deload sleep to a full night, not a cram night.

**Day 1 — exam day**
10. Carb-rich familiar breakfast (rice + egg, or bread + banana). Water only. No caffeine experiment.
11. Nothing new. Same breakfast, same time, whatever worked on previous school days.

**Day 0 to results**
12. Match day = high-carb day (8 g/kg the day before), recovery meal within 30 min, weigh before/after to calibrate your own sweat rate.
