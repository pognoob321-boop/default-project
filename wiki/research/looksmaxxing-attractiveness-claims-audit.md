# Looksmaxxing Attractiveness Claims Audit

## Summary

Audited 13 attractiveness/face-analysis claims from the looksmaxxing wiki against the primary literature. Headline: the numeric "ideal ratio" table is almost entirely forum-derived, not scientific. Three claims (Re and Rule 2016, golden ratio, symmetry) are real but routinely misstated.

Verdict tally: 3 SUPPORTED, 4 PARTLY SUPPORTED, 4 CONTRADICTED, 2 NO EVIDENCE FOUND.

## Verdicts

### 1. Re and Rule 2016, "BMI decrease of ~2.38 kg/m2" - PARTLY SUPPORTED (wrong sex, wrong label)

- Real paper: Re & Rule (2016), "Heavy Matters", Social Psychological and Personality Science 7(1):69-76. DOI 10.1177/1948550615599829.
- 1.33 kg/m2 = just-noticeable-difference for perceived weight, not attractiveness.
- 2.38 kg/m2 = women only. Men = 2.59 kg/m2.
- It is the size of loss where raters notice an attractiveness change. It is not "the threshold for attractiveness improvement".
- Optimal-BMI figure from the same line of work: ~19.1 kg/m2 female, ~23.8 kg/m2 male.
- The 2010 Perception paper cited by the wiki is real: Re & Rule (2010), "Normative body fat dynamics and the attractiveness of male facial traits", Perception 39(2):200-209.

### 2. "Skin quality beats masculinity as a predictor" - CONTRADICTED

- Foo, Simmons & Rhodes (2017), Scientific Reports 7:39731. DOI 10.1038/srep39731. In men, attractiveness was predicted positively by masculinity, symmetry and averageness, and negatively by adiposity. Skin colour/condition did not predict attractiveness in either sex.
- Skin apparent health does predict attractiveness (Jones, Little, Burt & Perrett 2004, PMID 15250662), with a large effect on perceived health. But it is not stronger than masculinity.
- The studies favouring skin colour over masculinity are weak, small, male-only, and were cited by Foo et al. as exceptions.

### 3. "Body fat 12-15% = max facial definition, 10-12% max" - NO EVIDENCE FOUND

- No peer-reviewed source sets a body-fat threshold for facial definition in men. The literature uses BMI or facial-morphometric manipulations, never a BF% threshold.
- What exists: Windhager, Patocka & Schaefer (2013), Am J Hum Biol - body fat explained 8.7% of facial shape variation in adolescent girls. de Jager et al. (2018), Front Psychol 9:2562 - meta-analysis, r = 0.71 for perceived facial adiposity vs BMI.
- Direction of effect is real; the specific numbers are gym-bro folklore.

### 4. Canthal tilt "ideal 6-8 deg" - CONTRADICTED as an ideal; population norm is higher

- Population norm: Lee, Choung & Choung (2020), J Korean Assoc Oral Maxillofac Surg 46(6):379-384. PMID 33377462. Mean canthal tilt 8.45 +/- 3.53 deg (right), 8.42 +/- 3.81 deg (left), n=76 Korean young adults. Zero participants had negative tilt. Class I = 0-5 deg, class II = more than 5 deg.
- Clinical reference: Hantash & Gladstone (2010) - lateral canthus should sit ~2 mm above medial in men, up to 4 mm in women.
- One attractiveness-relevant study: Bashour & Geist (2007), Ophthalmic Plast Reconstr Surg 23(1):52-56. PMID 17237692. Forced choice preferred accentuated medial canthal tilt 93% of the time - female faces only, no degree target, no normal comparison.
- 2025 evidence points the other way: elevating the lateral canthus reduced attractiveness and raised perceived threat (JPRAS 2025, 104:417-425).
- No paper by "Yehezkel" on canthal tilt or facial attractiveness could be located. Likely a mis-citation.
- Conclusion: 6-8 deg is looksmax.org consensus and sits below the measured population mean of ~8.5 deg. It is not an empirically derived optimum.

### 5. Facial thirds / fifths - CONTRADICTED as normative; 33/35/33 is close to real-world data

- Farkas, Hreczko, Kolar & Munro (1985), Plast Reconstr Surg 75(3):328-338. PMID 3883374. Tested 9 neoclassical canons on 153 young adults. Vertical canons were the worst fitting. Middle third was smallest in both North American White and African American samples; lower third exceeded middle third in 100% of both groups.
- Equal middle/lower thirds occurred in only 5.4% of Saudi men (Al Refaie et al. 2015, Head Face Med, DOI 10.1186/s13005-015-0064-y).
- Attractive faces measured 3.13 (women) vs 3.38 (men) facial-height-to-forehead-height, i.e. men deviate further from equal thirds (PMC10325763).
- 33/35/33 is not forum-invented - it matches real-world means better than 33/33/33. But framing it as an "ideal" is wrong: Farkas et al. state the canons "do not represent the average facial proportions and their interpretation as a prescription for ideal facial proportions must be tested."
- Asymmetry is universal: Ferrario et al. (1995) found mean faces of 80 healthy adults were significantly asymmetric in shape, symmetric in size.
- Fifths: same Farkas 1985 paper - nose width = 1/4 face width held in only 37% of young adults.

### 6. Specific ratio numbers - one partly supported, the rest forum-derived

| (b) tFWHR 1.33-1.38 | no evidence found | No literature on a "total facial width-to-height ratio" ideal. Not a standard anthropometric or aesthetic measure. |
| (c) ESR 44-47% of bizygomatic | PARTLY SUPPORTED | Pallett, Link & Lee (2010), Vision Research 50(2):149-154. DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2009.11.003. Optimal interocular distance = 46% of face width. | (d) Gonial angle 112-125 deg | population norm, not ideal | Jensen (1954), Angle Orthod - normal range 100-148 deg. Forensic studies: males 124.7 deg (51-60y), 124.1 deg (61-70y). Population means exceed the wiki upper bound. |

But the population norm is far higher: Fitzgerald, Nanda & Currier (1992), AJODO 102(4):328-334 - 114 +/- 10 deg. Literature "normal" range 90-120 deg. |

| (g) Midface ratio 0.98-1.02 | NO EVIDENCE FOUND | No published definition or norm for a "midface ratio" as an attractiveness target. Villavisanis et al. (2023), Ann Plast Surg 91(2):287-293 used "middle third ratio" only as a masculinity correlate, n=827. |

### 7. Sexual dimorphism in male attractiveness - PARTLY SUPPORTED, effect small and curvilinear

- Dixson, Sulikowski, Gouda-Vossos, Rantala & Brooks (2016), J Evol Biol 29(11):2311-2320. DOI 10.1111/jeb.12958. N=8520. Best single estimate: both masculinized and feminized faces were less attractive than unmanipulated faces; the effect was small and did not reverse the attractiveness ordering.
- Hodgson & Chan (2019/2020), Arch Sex Behav, PMID 31016490 - n=769, 3D faces. Condition-dependent variables had very small effects, if any.
- Lee et al. (2025), Sci Rep - masculinity did not predict attractiveness of male faces.
- Foo et al. (2017) - masculinity did predict. Kleisner et al. (2021), Evol Hum Sci, five populations - masculinity predicted attractiveness in only 2 of 5.
- DeBruine, Jones, Smith & Little (2010), JEP:HPP 36(3):751-758, DOI 10.1037/a0016457 - after masking non-face confounds, masculine vs feminine preference differences disappeared.
- Verdict: weak-to-null in modern large-N work. Not a lever you can push meaningfully.

### 8. Symmetry-attractiveness correlation - PARTLY SUPPORTED, weaker than folklore

- Rhodes (2006), Annu Rev Psychol 57:199-226. Meta-analysis: symmetry-attractiveness r = 0.30 (rated), 0.19 (measured).
- Weeden & Sabini (2005), Psychol Bull 131(5):635-653. Meta-analysis of 13 studies: symmetry had little effect on male and no effect on female facial attractiveness.
- Rhodes et al. (2007), Perception 36(8):1244-1252, PMID 17972486 - most of the symmetry-attractiveness link disappears when perceived health is controlled, i.e. it is largely a health cue, not a geometric preference.
- Moller & Thornhill (1998), Am Nat 151(2):174-192 - cross-species bilateral symmetry meta-analysis.

### 9. Averageness / composite faces - SUPPORTED

- Rhodes (2006) meta-analysis, 18 papers, mean effect size d = 0.52.
- Langlois, Kalakanis, Rubenstein, Larson, Hallam & Smoot (2000), Psychol Bull 126(3):390-423, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.126.3.390 - 11 meta-analyses.
- Lee et al. (2025), Sci Rep - averageness predicted attractiveness in both male and female faces.
- Caveat: composite/halved faces win on average across raters. Germine et al. (2015), Curr Biol 25(19):2680-2685 showed individual aesthetic preferences are dominated by environment, not genes.

### 10. "Attractive people are healthier" - PARTLY SUPPORTED, weak

- Weeden & Sabini (2005) - of all candidate cues, only female waist-hip ratio and weight predicted both attractiveness and health. Little indication that male attractiveness relates to male health.
- Langlois et al. (2000) - attractiveness-physical health d = +0.38 (5 studies).
- Foo, Simmons & Rhodes (2017) - weak links between attractive facial traits and health. Masculinity predicted semen quality; averageness did not.
- Bulczak & Gugushvili (2023), Am J Hum Biol 35(8):e23895, PMID 36932650, DOI 10.1002/ajhb.23895 - Add Health, n~15,000, interviewer-rated attractiveness, robust association with 10-year cardiometabolic risk, adjusted for initial health and SES. Causation not established.
- Verdict: real but small epidemiological correlation, strongest in women, confounded by SES and behaviour.

### 11. FWHR to dominance / testosterone - PARTLY SUPPORTED, with major replication problems

Supporting:
- Lefevre, Lewis, Perrett & Penke (2013), Evol Hum Behav 34:273-279 - fWHR associated with testosterone in men.
- Lefevre et al. (2014), Biol Lett 10:20140729 - fWHR predicted self-reported dominance and aggression in both sexes.
- Valentine, Li, Penke & Perrett (2014), Psychol Sci 25(3):806-811, DOI 10.1177/0956797613511823 - speed dating; fWHR to perceived dominance to short-term attractiveness.

Contradicting / limiting:
- Kordsmeyer, Freund, Pita, Jünger & Penke (2019) - fWHR and global facial masculinity are not positively associated with testosterone levels.
- Lefevre et al. (2014), PMC4831733 - fWHR not associated with adolescent testosterone. Only the other ratios tracked T, strength and voice pitch.
- Kramer, Jones & Ward (2012), PLoS ONE - lack of sexual dimorphism in fWHR in White European faces across 2D, 3D and anthropometry.
- Özener (2011), Evol Hum Behav 33:169-173, DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2011.08.001 - fWHR not sexually dimorphic and unrelated to aggression in a Turkish sample.
- PMC4793305 - within-person fWHR variability is large relative to between-person; 2D-photo fWHR measurement is noisy.
- PMC9554718 - bizygomatic width is the strongly dimorphic measure (d = 1.39), not fWHR.
- Verdict: heavily cited, frequently cited against itself. Unsettled and population-dependent.

### 12. Golden ratio phi = 1.618 - CONTRADICTED as an aesthetic law

- Pallett, Link & Lee (2010), Vision Research 50(2):149-154, DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2009.11.003. The optimum is not phi. Optimal eye-mouth distance = 36% of face length; optimal interocular distance = 46% of face width. Preferred ratios were significantly different from the classic golden section (t(2) = -8.63, p < .05). These "new golden ratios" are simply the average face's proportions.
- Green (1995), Perception 24:937-968, PMID 8848362 - little support for the golden ratio.
- Holland et al. - golden proportions do not predict male facial attractiveness.
- Wang et al. (2011), Int J Oral Maxillofac Surg - 3D ratios not related to attractiveness; 7 of 10 ratios significantly different from 1.618.
- Zwahlen, Tang, Leung & Tan (2022), Maxillofac Plast Reconstr Surg 44:28, DOI 10.1186/s40902-022-00358-2 - 77 measurements tested; only 6 ratios, 8 angles, 1 canon and 1 proportion correlated significantly with 3D attractiveness ratings.
- Marquardt Phi Mask is a commercial device, not a validated instrument.

### 13. "Third eyeball rule" and "facial fifths" as attractiveness determinants - NO EVIDENCE FOUND / CONTRADICTED

- No peer-reviewed study validates the third eyeball rule (eyes one eye-width apart) as an attractiveness determinant.
- looksmax.org itself concedes it: the "Ideal facial ratios" thread says the rule is "widely accepted ... however this is flawed and incorrect".
- Facial fifths, weak support only: Milutinovic et al. (2014), Scientific World Journal 2014:428250 - attractive females showed statistically equal thirds and fifths while the non-attractive control group did not (small n, females only, no effect size). Against it: Farkas et al. (1985) - the nose-width/face-width canon held in only 37% of young adults; the Arabian Peninsula study found the orbital canon failed in 71% of women.
- The real published equivalent is the EME angle: Danel & Pawlowski (2007), J Comp Psychol 121(2):221-225, PMID 17516801 - EME angle is sexually dimorphic, size-independent, and negatively correlated with male facial attractiveness.

## Takeaways

1. Rewrite features/facial-ratios.md to label every row as community consensus (looksmax.org), not research.
2. Add a standing warning at the top of that page: population norm is not an attractiveness optimum. Nasolabial angle is the cleanest example - the ideal (93-98 deg) is about 15 deg below the norm (105-115 deg).
3. Fix the Re and Rule citation: 2.38 is women, 2.59 is men, and 1.33 is the perception threshold. Neither is an improvement threshold.
4. Delete or clearly label as folklore the 12-15% body-fat facial-definition claim.
5. Canthal tilt: replace "ideal 6-8 deg" with "population mean about 8.5 deg (Korean young adults, n=76)". Do not claim an optimum exists.
6. Keep, with caveats attached: symmetry, averageness, fWHR as a dominance signal.
7. Delete: golden ratio law, third eyeball rule, tFWHR ideal, chin-to-philtrum ideal, midface ratio ideal.
