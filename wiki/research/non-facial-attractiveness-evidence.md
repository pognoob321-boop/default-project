# Non-Facial Attractiveness Evidence

> The companion to [looksmaxxing-attractiveness-claims-audit](looksmaxxing-attractiveness-claims-audit.md).
> That page audited the *face*. This one audits everything else: body, voice, height,
> scent, movement, teeth, personality, status, and the halo effect.
>
> Method note: `concepts/appealmaxxing.md` previously listed these as a bulleted wish list with
> zero citations. Everything below is graded **SUPPORTED / PARTLY / CONTRADICTED / NO EVIDENCE**
> against the primary literature, with effect sizes where they exist.

## Summary

**The answer to "how do I get more appeal" is: muscle, then voice, then personality — and not the face.**

Three findings dominate everything else:

1. **Muscularity is the only masculine trait with consistent evidence for both mating *and* reproduction** in a 96-study meta-analysis of 177,044 men. Facial masculinity is **not significant** for either.
2. **Height is close to a non-lever.** Its association with mating is r = .057, and real couples show it far more weakly than people claim to prefer.
3. **Personality assortatively mates more strongly than height does** (conscientiousness r = .16, agreeableness r = .11 across 199 studies) — and those traits predict *actual relationship satisfaction*, not just photos.

The most-repeated appealmaxxing tactics online — peacocking, negging, cosmetic surgery, scent chemistry — have **zero or null** support.

## The anchor: Lidborg, Cross & Boothroyd (2022)

eLife 11:e65031. PMID 35179485, PMC9106334. 96 studies, 474 effect sizes, N = 177,044 unique participants,
99 samples across industrialised and non-industrialised populations. First meta-analysis ever to
pool facial masculinity, body masculinity, 2D:4D, voice pitch, height and testosterone against both
mating *and* reproductive outcomes.

| Trait | → mating (r) | 95% CI | → reproduction | → offspring viability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Body masculinity (strength/muscle)** | **.133** | [.091, .176] | **consistent** | ns |
| Voice pitch (lower) | .132 | [.061, .204] | ns | ns |
| Testosterone | .093 | [.066, .121] | ns | ns |
| Facial masculinity | .080 | [−.003, .164] | — | ns |
| Height | .057 | [.027, .087] | ns | ns |
| 2D:4D finger ratio | .034 | q = .102 | — | — |

- Facial masculinity **failed to reach significance** (q = .117). This is the third independent
  large-N failure to find a reliable masculinity→attractiveness effect, after Dixson 2016
  (N = 8520, "the masculinity paradox") and Kleisner 2021 (predicted in only 2 of 5 populations).
- Muscularity was the only trait predicting reproduction, and only in **non-contracepting populations**.
  The authors explicitly call for testing outside industrialised populations.
- Note the confound: they excluded analyses without fat/muscle proportion, so "body masculinity"
  here is genuine lean mass and strength, not BMI.

**What this means:** if you want the single most evidence-backed physical lever, it is
[frame-gym-training](../features/frame-gym-training.md) — upper body especially — not
[facial-ratios](../features/facial-ratios.md).

## Voice

**Verdict: PARTLY SUPPORTED, and mostly not controllable.**

- Male F0 (pitch) negatively predicts male vocal attractiveness, **curvilinear**. Smith et al. (2020),
  PMC7719159, n = 160 male speakers with biomarkers: negative linear *and* negative quadratic
  relationships, F(2,157) = 4.38, p = .038 for short-term attractiveness. R² = .17 short-term,
  .09 long-term. The curvilinear model fits better than linear, i.e. **very low pitch is not better.**
- Replicated across cultures: PM11076608, n ≈ 125 voices — F0 negatively correlated with male voice
  attractiveness (β = −.211, p < .001) and *positively* with female voice attractiveness
  (β = .069, p = .040). Suire et al. 2019 (n = 165 judges, 58 stimuli): a voice 1 SD lower in F0
  wins up to **65%** of forced choices (χ² = 24.89, p < .001).
- **BUT pitch is a poor proxy for what it is supposed to signal.** Papesh et al. meta-analysis
  (39 samples, 5 continents): formant-based vocal-tract-length estimates independently explained
  **10%** of height within sexes; **F0 explained only 2% of men's height** and 0.5% of women's.
  All but one vocal-tract-length measure predicted body size better than pitch.
- **And formants, the better size cue, do not predict attractiveness.** PM11076608: no significant
  correlation with vocal tract length. Pisanski & Rendall 2011 (3 experiments): when pitch and
  formants are put in conflict, listeners track **formants** for size, masculinity *and* attractiveness —
  so the two cues genuinely disagree, and the one that predicts body size is the one that fails to
  predict attractiveness.
- **Averaging your voice does nothing.** Same paper: vocal averageness significantly reduced
  distinctiveness (manipulation worked) but produced **no** increase in attractiveness.
- **Loudness is a real, modifiable lever.** Suire 2019: intensity significant overall
  (χ² = 7.52, p = .006); older women preferred louder voices (interaction χ² = 5.65, p = .017).
  F0-SD (pitch variability — expressiveness, not monotonicity) was the second strongest
  predictor, χ² = 34.00, p < .001.

**Actionable:** you cannot meaningfully lower testosterone-driven pitch, and chasing a very low
pitch is counterproductive (inverted U). What you *can* control is **projection/loudness and pitch
variability** — speak louder and with more dynamic range. The wiki's "proper posture maximises
vocal projection" claim in [puberty-optimization](../features/puberty-optimization.md) is
consistent with the intensity finding.

## Body composition

**Verdict: SUPPORTED — and the current dogma about ratios is wrong.**

- **Adiposity beats ratios.** Barber (2025), *Physiology & Behavior*, S0191886925002028. 15 DXA
  body images (faces blurred, height not given) rated in **China, Lithuania and the UK**. The
  BF%-attractiveness relationship is **inverted-U**, as is BMI, with the optimum around
  **BMI 23-27** (evolutionary model predicted 23.2-24.8). **Adiposity explained more variance than
  shoulder-to-waist ratio in all three countries**; in China and Lithuania SWR failed to reach
  significance once adiposity was in the model. SWR peaked around 1.57 but the curve was flat.
- **This contradicts the classic V-taper literature.** Maisey et al. 1999 (*Lancet*) reported
  waist-to-chest ratio explained **56%** of variance vs BMI's 12.7%; Swami et al. 2007
  (Greece + Britain) again found WCR primary. Both used photographs of real men where WCR and BMI
  covary. Barber separated them on DXA and adiposity won. Prefer the separated study.
- **For men, "attractive" body composition ≈ "healthy" body composition.** Brierley et al. 2016,
  PMC4892674, n = 63 raters manipulating apparent fat and muscle independently: for **women**,
  observers chose fat mass well below the healthy range; for **men**, the optimal fat and muscle
  chosen for attractiveness was **inside the healthy range with no significant difference from the
  health optimum**. Cultural thin-ideals distort female judgements and do not distort male ones.
  This is a genuine sex difference and it matters — do not port "get leaner" advice from female
  literature onto men.
- **Which muscles matter.** Sell/Boothroyd-group study, PMC10480816, N = 1,742 (Spain). Women's
  preferred size ranking: obliques > glutes > abdominals > biceps > shoulders > triceps > calves >
  deltoids > quadriceps > pectoralis > latissimus > forearms > tibialis anterior > trapezius.
  Women showed **no** overall upper-vs-lower size preference (p = .361) — but on the dichotomous
  "does this muscle affect men's attractiveness?" question, **every upper-body muscle cleared 50%
  and almost no lower-body muscle did** (except glutes). So: upper body as a *category* carries
  the signal.
- **Muscularity has an inverted-U too.** Frederick, Swami & International Body Project 2010,
  41 samples / 26 countries: women preferred above-average muscularity nearly everywhere, but
  **very low and very high muscle were both dispreferred**.
- **Male raters prefer more muscle than female raters do** — in all 14 muscles tested, men rated
  bigger muscles more attractive (PMC10480816). Worth knowing if your target audience is unclear.

## Height

**Verdict: SUPPORTED but tiny, and it is not a lever after growth.**

- Lidborg 2022: height → mating r = **.057**. Significant, barely.
- Stulp et al. 2013, *PLOS ONE* 8:e54186 (Millennium Cohort Study). Real couples vs random-mating
  simulation: assortative mating **r = .18** only; male-taller norm violated **26% less** than
  chance; male-not-too-tall (>25 cm) violated **12% less** than chance. The authors: preferences
  "are only weakly translated into actual couple formation." Preference slopes (.77 for women)
  were **four times** the realised slopes (.19).
- Stulp et al. 2013, *Anim Behav* 86:37-46, 5,782 speed-daters, 128,104 choices. Women most often
  chose a man **25 cm** taller; men most often chose a woman only **7 cm** shorter. Matches peaked
  at a **19 cm** difference — optimal for *neither* sex. Sexual conflict over height produces
  systematically suboptimal pairings.
- **The threshold is "average or above," not "taller."** Barkow 2014, *Am J Hum Biol* 26:530-537,
  N = 165,606 (US IHIS), segmented regression: the gains run short → average; average → tall are
  marginal. "Being tall is less important than not being short."
- Xu et al. 2018 found **no** significant association between men's height and offspring number
  across 16 studies.

**Actionable:** maximize reaching your genetic height potential (sleep, protein, training — see
[puberty-optimization](../features/puberty-optimization.md)), then stop. Do not buy elevator
schemes for the payoff; buy them only for [appearance-frasms](../features/appearance-frauds.md) if
you want them, where the return is social optics, not measured mating.

## Scent

**Verdict: CONTRADICTED. The "chemical attraction" story does not survive meta-analysis.**

- Havlíček, Winternitz & Roberts 2020, *Phil Trans R Soc B*, doi 10.1098/rstb.2019.0260
  (PMC7209936). Four meta-analyses: genomic mate selection in actual couples — **null**;
  relationship satisfaction — **null**; odour preference — **null**; overall combined estimate —
  **null**. They also document **publication bias** in the positive literature. This directly
  contradicts Wedekind et al. 1995 (*Proc R Soc B*), the T-shirt study that the entire MHC-attraction
  claim rests on.
- Direction is contested at the level of a reversal: Roberts et al. (*Science*, S0031938418311995)
  found real couples' **unfragranced** odours were rated **more similar** than random pairings —
  i.e. *assortative*, the opposite of the disassortative prediction — **and that adding fragrance
  eliminated the effect.**
- Thornhill et al. 2003, *Behav Ecol* 14:668: the two olfactory systems are independent. Women showed
  no MHC trait preference at any point in the cycle; **men** preferred MHC-similar.
- What survives: Milinski et al. 2013 (PMC3574394) showed humans *can* detect MHC peptide ligands in
  their own axillary odour (n = 17, z = −2.394, p = .0167; effect vanished with a cold or smoking).
  Self-recognition, not attraction.

**Actionable:** hygiene and not over-masking. There is a defensible reading that heavy fragrance
*destroys* a real signal, so a clean, light scent is the low-risk choice. But do not expect a
"compatibility" effect — the evidence is null, and the one study where it mattered most showed the
opposite of the popular claim.

## Movement and posture

**Verdict: SUPPORTED that dynamic cues matter — but the male-specific data is thin.**

- Dance and gait are independently rated for attractiveness, and dance–gait attractiveness
  correlate (lay assessors ρ = .51, p < .001; dance experts ρ = .40, p = .11).
- Rater expertise matters: dance experts rated the same movements more attractive than lay
  assessors (F(1,2347) = 14.17, p < .001). And **gaits were rated more attractive than dances**
  (F(1,2316) = 75.85, p < .001; mean diff 0.47) — ordinary walking beats performed movement.
- Dynamic cues are **not** weaker than static ones for attractiveness (Morrison & Gheorghiu 2025,
  100 participants, motion capture; photos vs video vs motion-capture stimuli, doi
  10.1080/00224545.2025.2580295). Morrison et al. 2018 had already found dynamic cues as important
  as static for judging female walkers.
- Alignment matters: when sex information matches the dynamic cues, ratings of attractiveness,
  competence and warmth all increase (Kader, Morrison & Gheorghiu 2025, *Acta Psychologica*,
  doi 10.1016/j.actpsy.2025.106201, 2 × n = 36).
- **Evidence gap:** the biomechanical gait-attractiveness literature (PMC11504962, PMC9932886)
  is almost entirely **female** — hip energy, lumbar curvature, arm take-back, knee extension.
  There is no comparable male parameterisation. Treat "male gait attractiveness" as unstudied.

## Teeth and smile

**Verdict: SUPPORTED — and the ranking is different for men than for dentists.**

- Al Hamad et al. 2021, *J Esthet Restor Dent*, doi 10.1111/jerd.12834, n = 1,040 raters
  (laypeople, dental students, dentists, specialists), 13 manipulated parameters, α = .861:
  - **Large** effects (laypeople / dental): tooth **colour** (−0.56 / −0.56) and **diastema**
    (−0.55 / −0.54).
  - **Medium**: midline shift (−0.37 / −0.49), midline cant (−0.36 / −0.48), occlusal plane cant
    (−0.36 / −0.49), high smile (−0.42 / −0.51 for lay/dental), incisor edge symmetry
    (−0.46 / −0.54).
  - **Small**: gingival margin level, teeth width proportion, inverted smile arch, commissure cant.
- **Laypeople cannot see the orthodontic details dentists chase.** Kokich, Kiyak & Shapiro 2000:
  general dentists and laypeople could not detect even a **4 mm** maxillary midline deviation;
  all groups needed only 2 mm of incisor angulation. Alsulaimani & Batwa: all three groups had
  **zero tolerance** for central incisor length change; laypeople tolerated lateral incisor
  reductions to a 48-61% width ratio.
- Width is real but **only where you can see it**: Horn et al. 2021 (PMC7854600), n = 613 young
  adults with 3D imaging, +10.26% self-rated attractiveness per 10% increase in proportional smile
  width — **but the effect was null in males** (adjusted R² = −0.008, p = .612) and present only
  in females.
- Orthodontic treatment itself: systematic review (PMC9899877) — moderately positive but **9 of 10
  studies at serious risk of bias**, heterogeneity too large to meta-analyse. Do not cite this as
  evidence for orthodontics.

**Actionable:** tooth colour is the highest-yield dental change for a man; whitening is on
[appearance-frauds](../features/appearance-frauds.md) and holds up. Sub-millimetre incisor
proportion work is invisible to non-professionals — skip it.

## Personality and behaviour

**Verdict: SUPPORTED, and underrated relative to how much effort people spend on their jaw.**

- Barelds 2010, *J Res Pers*, 19 samples / 3,848 participants: **low neuroticism, high
  agreeableness, high conscientiousness, high extraversion** all significantly predict partner's
  relationship satisfaction, and none of it varies by sex or marital status.
- Horwitz et al. 2023, PMC10967253 — 480 partner correlations from 199 studies, plus 133 traits
  across up to 79,074 UK Biobank couples. Assortative correlations: conscientiousness **r = .16**,
  openness **r = .21**, agreeableness **r = .11**, neuroticism **r = .11**, extraversion **r = .08**.
  Compare anthropometrics: height r = .27, BMI r = .25, WHR r = .19 (UKB). Attitudinal traits are
  far higher: political values **r = .58**, education high.
- Read that carefully: **we assort on values and education far more strongly than on bodies.**
- Botwin, Buss & Shackelford 1997: people prefer mates similar to themselves and actually obtain
  mates who match what they wanted; dissatisfaction was most predicted by a partner being **lower
  than desired on Agreeableness, Emotional Stability, and Openness**.
- Atari et al. 2019 (Iran, Pakistan, Turkey, N = 1,089): **agreeableness consistently predicted
  preference for kind and dependable partners across all three cultures**, while other
  personality correlates were variable across cultures. One trait, one country cluster, but the
  only non-Western data here.
- Signalling caveat: Komatsu et al. 2024 (n = 24) found interest in a prospective partner was best
  explained by **perceived** personality similarity (R² > .80) rather than actual similarity —
  i.e. what people *think* you are beats what you are, at first contact. Small sample, but it
  points the same direction as the halo effect below.

**Actionable:** conscientiousness and agreeableness are the two with both a partner-assortation
effect and an independent relationship-satisfaction effect. That is a years-long investment, not a
quarter.

## The halo effect

**Verdict: SUPPORTED, with an important and under-reported limitation.**

- Langlois et al. 2000, *Psychol Bull* 126:390-423, 11 meta-analyses: raters agree on attractiveness
  within and across cultures; attractive people are **judged more positively, treated more
  positively (even by those who know them), and exhibit more positive behaviours.**
- Hosoda, Stone-Romero & Coats 2003, *J Organ Behav* (doi 10.1111/j.1744-6570.2003.tb00157.x):
  experimental job-outcome studies, weighted mean **d = .37**. The bias did not shrink when
  job-relevant information was provided, and professionals were as susceptible as students.
- **The limitation that matters: the halo is not free money, and it is competence-shaped.**
  Eagly et al. 1991 (11 meta-analyses of the stereotype) found the effect **largest for social
  competence, intermediate for potency/adjustment/intellectual competence, and near ZERO for
  integrity and concern for others.** Looking better changes how competent you seem, not how kind
  you seem. Anyone using appearance to assume warmth will be disappointed.
- Griffin & Langlois 2006: the direction is usually **negativity bias** — being unattractive is the
  disadvantage, being attractive is a modest advantage. Both halves matter for how you set
  expectations.
- Hosoda also found the bias **decreasing in recent years**.

## Culture

**Verdict: MIXED — and one famous theory is dead.**

- Buss 1989, *Behav Brain Sci* (doi 10.1017/S0140525X00023992), 37 samples / 33 countries /
  **N = 10,047**: women valued "good financial prospect" more than men in **36 of 37 samples**
  (Spain the exception, right direction, non-significant). Indonesia t = −9.46; Japan t = −15.97;
  China t = −5.34.
- Walter et al. 2020, *Psychol Sci*, **45 countries, N = 14,399**: the core sex differences replicate
  robustly. But **neither pathogen prevalence nor gender equality robustly predicted them.**
- Zhang et al. 2019, *Evol Psychol* 17(2), PMC10481077, **36 countries, N = 3,073**: the
  gender-equality moderation **"we found little evidence"** — one suggestive analysis did not
  survive control for Galton's problem or multiple comparisons.
- **"Structural powerlessness" is a retracted-by-its-author position.** Zentner 2015,
  *Pers Soc Psychol Rev* (doi 10.1177/1088868315585436) documents that **Buss himself rejected**
  the concept (and its predictions) in Buss 1989 and Buss 2011. Do not cite it as consensus.
- **Skin preference genuinely reverses by culture.** Lu et al. 2021, *PLOS ONE* 16:e0259276:
  L\* → attractiveness was significant **only** for Chinese observers rating Chinese faces
  (p = .002); Western Europeans preferred *decreased redness* and *increased yellowness*.
  Han & Stephen: UK β = +0.45 toward yellowness, China β = −1.01 away — opposite directions on
  the same axis. Zhan et al. 2021, *J Vision* 21:2739 (40 East Asian + 40 Western European faces):
  an SVM cleanly separated the two preference models.
  **Practical read for a Cambodian male:** this region's baseline already prefers lighter skin —
  a feature you have by default. Tanning is a Western preference and may work *against* you here.
- **Myth kill: double eyelids are a shared preference, not an East-vs-West divergence.** Hwang &
  Spiegel 2014, PMID 24604790: 19 digitally-altered Chinese-descent models rated by Chinese and
  non-Chinese observers — a **medium-height crease was most attractive in both groups**
  (p < .00001), an absent crease least attractive in both.
- **Southeast Asian evidence gap — confirmed.** There are no standalone peer-reviewed quantitative
  mate-appearance samples from Vietnam, Thailand, Cambodia, Myanmar, Laos or the Philippines in the
  mainstream literature. Indonesia (in Buss 1989) and Malaysia (Swami et al. 2010,
  doi 10.1177/0146167209359702) exist; those countries appear elsewhere only as single sites inside
  mega-studies. **Everything above is extrapolated.** Flagged so nobody later mistakes it for local data.
- **Ideals are socially transmitted, not innate.** Grieco, Zhang & Roberts 2006 (PMC3013470):
  pairing attractive models with wide-eye-spacing targets made **novel** individuals more likely to
  be judged as having wide eye spacing — the mechanism by which a preference spreads through a
  population. Chen, Robinson & Jablonski 2019 (doi 10.1093/tbm/ibz063, n = 48 across three
  Chinese-diaspora groups): adoptees raised by Euro-American parents preferred tanned skin and
  sun-seeked; mainland-migrant women preferred lighter skin and sun-protected. Kowal et al. 2022:
  individuals from less gender-equal countries spent more time on appearance enhancement
  (β = −0.186, p < .001).
- **Counterweight:** Western media exposure relates only *weakly* to female muscularity preferences
  across cultures (Frederick & Swami 2010; Gallup et al. 2010 review, PMC2850180) even while it
  robustly shifts body-size ideals (d = 1.94-2.49 for SES-graded preferences in Malaysia).
  Media moves the needle on ideals, unevenly by trait.

## Status, effort, and the "try harder" question

**Verdict: mixed — and the honest answer is that effort mostly fails to move mating outcomes.**

- **Men already invest almost as much as women.** Kowal et al. 2022, *Evol Hum Behav* 43:455-474,
  **N = 93,158 across 93 countries**: **99%** of people spend >10 min/day on appearance-enhancing
  behaviour. Women **238 min/day**, men **215 min/day** — a 23-minute gap. Excluding exercise:
  211 vs 182. Men spent more time on exercise specifically (β = −0.139, p < .001).
  The "men don't put in the effort" premise is false.
- **Money works, but only asymmetrically.** Hitsch, Hortaçsu & Ariely 2010, *AER* 100:130-163
  (millions of US daters): for men, no income effect below $50k, then monotonic — **+32%** more
  first contacts at higher income, up to **+156%** above $250k. College degree **+35%**. For women,
  income was at most marginally related and higher income was **no better**; postgraduate women
  actually saw a slight penalty.
- **Causal confirmation of the asymmetry.** Kirkebøen et al. 2021 (NBER w28688, Norway,
  admission-discontinuity design): marginal admission to an elite professional programme propelled
  **women** into elite household formation — higher own earnings, higher-earning partners, top
  income percentiles, **lower fertility**. Marginal admission for **men changed nothing.**
- **Dissenting evidence on assortative mating trends:** Gihleb & Lang 2016 (NBER w22927) find
  educational homogamy has **not** increased; the earlier estimates were an artefact of method.
  Eika, Mogstad & Zafar 2014 (NBER w20271) find educational assortative mating raised the US
  household-income Gini ~5%. Whether assortative mating has *risen* is genuinely contested.
- **Better messages perform worse.** Bruch & Newman 2018, *Sci Adv* 4:eap9815, 4 US cities:
  men wrote more positive messages and got **lower** reply rates (p < .001). Men are >2× more
  likely to get a reply from women *less* desirable than themselves; to more-desirable women the
  reply rate never exceeded **21%**. Both sexes aim ~25% above their own level.
- **Cosmetic surgery: null or trivial.** Zimm et al. 2013, *JAMA Facial Plast Surg*
  (doi 10.1001/jamafacial.2013.268), 49 patients, 50 blinded raters: attractiveness change
  **+0.08/10, P > .54 — not significant**; authors conclude surgery "did not consistently improve
  their attractiveness." Parsa et al. 2019 (doi 10.1001/jamafacial.2019.0463), 24 men, 145 raters:
  +0.29/7 [0.13, 0.46] overall — small, and **masculinity +0.13 [−0.03, 0.30] not significant**,
  **chin augmentation null on every outcome**. Both are photo-rating proxies; neither measures a
  partner, a marriage, or anyone's satisfaction.
- **Peacocking and negging: no evidence.** A peer-reviewed review surveying the community
  literature (PMC10429087, *The Dating Mind*) states that many community strategies are unsupported,
  specifically: "so far there is no direct evidence in support of this particular behaviour"
  (peacocking) and "there is currently no direct evidence that 'negging' is universally effective."
  The two most-cited appealmaxxing levers have no support.
- **Mate-choice copying: real, but smaller and shakier than folklore.** Gouda-Vossos et al. 2018,
  *Adapt Hum Behav Physiol* 4:364-386 (doi 10.1007/s40750-018-0099-y): women rate male targets as
  more desirable alongside another woman; **no obvious effect for male choice**; under
  "augmentation" methods the sex difference disappears. Moderate publication bias.
  **Replication failure on mechanism:** Street et al. 2018 (PMC5788917) used *real* peer social
  information and found ratings of men's faces were influenced no more or less than images of
  **hands and abstract art** — "evidence for domain-specific social learning mechanisms in humans
  is weaker than previously suggested."
  In non-human species the effect has also shrunk under bias correction: Santos et al. 2026,
  *Biol Rev* — Hedges' g 0.45, OR 1.81, down from g 0.58 / OR 2.71 after correction.

## Unverifiable

- **"Gul & Epps 2007"** on inequality and status pursuit is widely cited in forums. Four independent
  search routes plus Crossref and Semantic Scholar returned no matching record. Treat as a probable
  mis-citation; the nearby real papers are Cottrell, Neuberg & Li 2007 (*JPSP* 92:208,
  doi 10.1037/0022-3514.92.2.208) and Gul & Uskul 2007 on anti-effeminacy bias. **Do not cite it.**
- Blake, Bastian, Denson et al. 2018 (*PNAS* 115:8722-8727, sexy selfies in 113 nations) was
  verified only as a secondary citation inside Blake & Brooks 2019. Open before relying on it.

## Practical ranking

What the evidence supports, in order, for a male in Cambodia:

| 2 | Conscientiousness + agreeableness | r = .16 / .11 assortative; predicts satisfaction independently | Years |
| 3 | Income / credentials | +32% to +156% contacts for men, causal evidence of asymmetry | Years |

| 5 | Vocal loudness + pitch variability | Significant effects; pitch itself mostly fixed | Weeks |

| 7 | Posture and walk | Dynamic cues matter as much as static; gaits beat dances | Weeks |
| — | Face ratios, mewing, chin implants | Facial masculinity ns; chin augmentation ns | Avoid |
| — | Peacocking, negging, scent chemistry | No evidence / meta-analytic null | Avoid |

## Evidence gaps

1. **No study measures actual long-term mate outcomes for male grooming, cosmetic surgery or
   appearance interventions.** Everything in that column is photo-rating proxy data. This is a
   hole in the literature, not a search failure.
2. **Southeast Asia is essentially unrepresented.** No VN/TH/KH/MM/LA/PH standalone samples.
3. **Male gait attractiveness is unstudied.** The biomechanical parameterisation is female-only.
4. **Population norm ≠ attractiveness optimum**, the same error the ratio table in
   [facial-ratios](../features/facial-ratios.md) makes — most of these studies measure one or the other.
