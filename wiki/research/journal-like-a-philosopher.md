# How to Journal (Like a Philosopher)

## Summary

Video gives a 4-piece journaling structure for days when words don't flow (~one page), then explains the philosophy behind it: humans are narrative beings, so deliberately authoring the story = self-improvement.

## The structure (write in this order)

1. **What happened** — a *purely factual* account of one notable event from today (or yesterday, if writing in the morning). Selective, NOT comprehensive, no judgment in it.
2. **Something you did well** — an achievement, small victory, or a habit you reliably fell back on. (Video's example: broke a work problem into smaller parts and solved the most important ones.)
3. **What did not go well** — problem areas, repeating mistakes ("besetting vices"), bad habits. Then diagnose: what conditions trigger it, what the thought-process pattern is, and where the reasoning goes wrong (example: anxiety spiral → map where the anxious mind errs, then reason with yourself).
4. **Zoom out** — not necessarily a paragraph; can run to pages. What does this event have in common with other events? Fit it into a larger narrative — give your life story internal logic.

## Philosophy behind step 4

- Charles Taylor, Alasdair MacIntyre, Marya Schechtman, Martin Heidegger: persons are storytelling beings; we construct a narrative as we live.
- Most of us do it on autopilot (habit/routine). Journaling = taking the *active, intentional* role: "If my life is a story I want to be its author."
- Julia Annas, *The Morality of Happiness*, ch. 1: "making sense of one's life as a whole." Ancient ethics starts with "Am I satisfied with my life as a whole?" — Aristotle, eudaimonia (flourishing). Achieving it requires reflective work on life as a whole, which is exactly what regular journaling provides.

## Setup (video's opening)

1. A journal you like. 2. A pen you like. 3. Just write — daily if possible, otherwise when you feel like it.
