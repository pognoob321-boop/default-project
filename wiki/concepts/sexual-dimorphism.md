# Sexual Dimorphism (in Facial Aesthetics)

> The differences in appearance between male and female faces that contribute to perceived attractiveness and masculine/feminine appeal.

## Definition

Sexual dimorphism refers to the physical differences between males and females of the same species beyond the sex organs. In facial aesthetics, it describes the balance of masculine and feminine features that contribute to attractiveness.

## Key Sexually Dimorphic Facial Features

### Masculine Features (High Dimorphism)
- Stronger, more prominent jawline
- Higher cheekbone projection
- Brow ridge prominence
- Thicker eyebrows
- Wider face relative to height
- More angular features
- Lower facial fat percentage
- More defined bone structure

### Feminine Features (Low Dimorphism)
- Softer, more refined jawline
- Larger eyes relative to face
- Higher forehead
- Thinner eyebrows
- Smaller nose
- Fuller lips
- Higher facial fat distribution
- More rounded features

## The Attractiveness Paradox

Research findings on dimorphism and attractiveness:

1. **Masculine features**: Often rated as attractive for short-term mating; signal genetic fitness
2. **Feminine features**: Often rated as attractive for long-term mating; signal warmth and trustworthiness
3. **Facial harmony**: Features working together matters more than individual dimorphism level
4. **Cultural variation**: Ideal dimorphism levels vary across cultures

## Community Terminology

- **High dimorphism** = "Chad" / "Mogger" appearance
- **Low dimorphism** = "Pretty boy" / "Soft" appearance
- **"Hunter eyes"**: Deep-set, hooded eyes (highly dimorphic)
- **"Neotenous" features**: Youthful, baby-faced (low dimorphism)

## The Reality

- Both high and low dimorphism can be attractive
- What matters is how features work together (harmony)
- ~~Skin condition often matters more than dimorphism level~~ **← CONTRADICTED**
- Real-life attractiveness includes non-physical factors
- Individual preference varies significantly

> ⚠️ **Verified 2026-10-14 — the dimorphism effect is weaker than this page
> implies, and one line is contradicted.**
>
> **The masculinity effect is weak-to-null in modern large-N work:**
> - **Dixson et al. 2016**, *J Evol Biol* (n=8520) — the "masculinity paradox":
>   both masculinized **and** feminized faces were rated **less** attractive
>   than unmodified faces. The effect was small and did not reverse the
>   attractiveness ordering.
> - **DeBruine, Jones, Smith & Little 2010** — after masking non-face
>   confounds, masculine-vs-feminine preference differences **disappeared**.
> - **Lee et al. 2025**, *Sci Rep* — masculinity did not predict male facial
>   attractiveness. **Foo et al. 2017** — it did. **Kleisner et al. 2021** across
>   five populations — predicted in only 2 of 5.
>
> **Practical read:** you cannot meaningfully "push" dimorphism as an
> attractiveness lever. This is the opposite of what the archetype system
> (`facial-structure-archetypes.md`) and the 4-pillar DIMO score
> (`four-pillars.md`) assume.
>
> **Contradicted:** the claim that skin condition beats dimorphism. Foo, Simmons
> & Rhodes 2017 (*Sci Rep* 7:39731) found masculinity predicted male
> attractiveness while **skin colour/condition did not predict it in either
> sex.** Skin apparent *health* does predict attractiveness (Jones et al. 2004,
> PMID 15250662) — but not more strongly than masculinity.
>
> **What does hold up:** averageness (d = 0.52, Rhodes 2006) and symmetry
> (r = 0.30 rated / 0.19 measured) — and even symmetry is largely a *health
> cue* that disappears when perceived health is controlled (Rhodes et al. 2007).
